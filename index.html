Six kinds of NASA data, one map

Every layer comes from a public NASA or USGS product. Nothing on the map is drawn by hand or made up.

Optical

The ground itself, down to 25 cm. Zoom from the whole crater to single boulders and dune ripples.

MRO HiRISE 25 cm, MRO CTX 6 m, Viking colour 232 m, served live by NASA Mars Trek
Topographic

Elevation, slope and roughness. Checked against 708 Perseverance positions: the heights agree within 0.8 m on average.

MRO CTX stereo elevation model, 20 m, USGS Astrogeology
Thermal

Loose sand versus bedrock. Ground that is coldest at night holds little heat, which marks sand and dust.

Mars Odyssey THEMIS night and day infrared, 100 m
Ground truth

What the rover has actually tested. 45 km of track and 30 sample tubes label the terrain for the predictor.

Mars 2020 traverse and waypoints, NASA sample catalogue
Atmospheric

Local time, daylight, sunlight and weather. Sun position from NASA’s Mars clock, sunlight from Beer’s law, air temperature and pressure from the rover’s weather station.

NASA GISS Mars24, NASA TM-102299, MEDA
Radiation

Solar storms headed for Mars. NASA’s storm simulations flag predicted arrivals, and each EVA gets a dose estimate.

NASA CCMC DONKI (WSA-ENLIL), Curiosity RAD

How it predicts the unexplored

The predictor is our team’s nearest-neighbour model, ported from Python and fed with real measurements instead of test data.

  1. Learn from the rover

    Each place Perseverance stopped is labelled with the geologic setting of that mission campaign: crater floor, delta, margin carbonates, inlet channel or rim.

  2. Compare from orbit

    For every 80 m cell of Jezero, six NASA measurements are compared with the rover-verified cells. The three closest matches vote, weighted by similarity.

  3. Test it blind

    Every known point is hidden together with all references within 1 km, then predicted from the rest. The map shows that score for your objective.

  4. Route around hazards

    A least-cost search finds the shortest passable route and the lowest-risk one, penalising slope, rough ground and loose sand.

The project

Astral Atlas answers one question: how can existing NASA data predict unknown geological and mineral features in Jezero Crater, and become a map for the astronauts who go there?

A crew picks a science goal and a starting point. The atlas ranks unvisited ground by how closely it matches places where Perseverance confirmed that kind of rock, checks which candidates can be reached inside the EVA limits, and compares a shortest route with a lowest-risk route. A live panel tracks local time, sunlight, the rover and solar storms, and turns them into a go or no-go.

Built by Team Astral Atlas for the NASA Space Apps Challenge. A planning aid and proof of concept, not flight software. Scores are relative evidence, not probabilities.
